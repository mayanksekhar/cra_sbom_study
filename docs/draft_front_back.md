Working title: From SBOM to finding: where correctness is lost in vulnerability handling under the Cyber Resilience Act

## Abstract
Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 makes SBOMs and vulnerability handling obligations for manufacturers of products with digital elements, and generators and scanners produce the artifacts that support them. Prior work compares generators on component accuracy. We ask whether an accurate SBOM is enough for correct vulnerability handling. For 53 project versions in five ecosystems we compare three generators (Syft, Trivy, cdxgen) against references built from each project's own package manager, score findings with Grype, use Trivy and OSV Scanner in controls, freeze the vulnerability databases, and attribute every missed finding to its cause. Of 1061 reference findings (scored with Grype), no miss traces to the scanner. The losses come from the SBOM and differ by generator and ecosystem: Syft recovers 43 of 201 on Maven. Scanners read the product's version from different places, so the same SBOM gives different findings. On nine Go applications analysed at product level, product scoped VEX statements removed 319 of 319 not called findings in Trivy and 0 of 323 in Grype, which honours only module scoped statements, and a module scoped statement from one product suppressed reachable findings in another (24 of 151 called findings). A static reachability oracle for Go classifies 323 of 496 findings (65 percent) as not called at product level [RESULT PENDING: agreement of that oracle with manual labels]; a first run over whole modules overstated what is called. We map the results to the regulation and to BSI TR-03183-2 and list what an evidence grade pipeline has to declare.

## 1 Introduction
Context [V, text read]: the regulation asks manufacturers to identify and document vulnerabilities and components, including by an SBOM in a commonly used machine readable format covering at the very least the top level dependencies (Annex I Part II point 1), and to handle vulnerabilities of the product including its components effectively (Article 13(8)). Reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities applies from 11 September 2026, the main obligations from 11 December 2027 (Article 71).
Question: is an accurate SBOM enough for correct vulnerability handling?
Approach: multi level references per ecosystem (declared, resolved, shipped, whole tree), a reference SBOM per project, the same scanner run on the reference and on each generator's SBOM, error attribution (component absent, no usable version, wrong version, scanner), controls with minimal SBOMs, and a Go reachability oracle for VEX.
Contributions, to confirm: (1) a method from SBOM to finding with miss attribution, applied to 53 project versions [V]; (2) measured failure modes: scope, lockfile dependence, version carriers, profiles, root identity [V, some on one or two projects]; (3) VEX behaviour of two scanners with controls and a cross product suppression measurement [V for Go]; (4) a mapping to the regulation and to BSI TR-03183-2 and a list of what a pipeline must declare [P, BSI from a mirror].
Structure: section 2 related work, 3 method, 4 and 5 results on SBOMs and scanners, 6 VEX and reachability, 7 what a pipeline must declare, 8 threats and limits.

## 2 Related work [P: six papers read in full, the others from snippets or abstracts, novelty checked against those six and one search for later work]
Generator accuracy. Mind the Gap (arXiv 2609.19920) compares Syft, Trivy and cdxgen on more than 3,000 JavaScript and Rust projects against a lockfile baseline, explains the divergences by scope, naming and provenance choices, finds empty SBOMs for Syft and Trivy without a lockfile, and covers SBOM generation only [read in full]. Python studies (arXiv 2409.06390, 2409.01214) report that generators change vulnerability scan results and identify causes of missing dependencies [snippets, O]. A study of Maven projects (Liu et al., FSE 2023) evaluates composition analysis tools on 21,130 modules [snippet, O].
SBOM based vulnerability management. Zhou, Dacier and Konstantinou (CODASPY 2026, arXiv 2511.20313) study 2,414 repositories in Python (Poetry), Rust, Ruby and PHP and report that Syft 1.33.0 and Trivy 0.66.0 give identical SBOMs from lock files that match the lock file ground truth (Jaccard 1.0, accuracy 100 percent), and that both produced empty SBOMs from package-lock.json at those versions. On 16 manually checked repositories (four per language) Grype and Trivy reported 113 vulnerabilities of which 9 were confirmed (false positive rate 92.0 percent), with near perfect agreement between the two scanners as stated, and a coverage based check removed 61.9 percent of the false ones. Separately, for 12 Go projects they report 121 vulnerabilities of which 31 had a listed affected symbol present in the compiled binary's symbol table (a reduction of 74.4 percent); that symbol table test is their Go reachability analysis, not a call graph analysis [read in full]. Our mechanical check (section 6.1) applies the same symbol table test to nine Go applications: 239 of 366 checkable findings have no vulnerable function in the built binary (65 percent), against 74.4 percent in their set; the projects and versions differ [V for our figure, results_go/mech_result_product.csv]. Two results of ours differ from theirs: Syft and Trivy list components from package-lock.json for our three npm projects, and in our 8 Rust projects Trivy lists fewer lockfile entries than Syft and cdxgen (9 to 55 per project). Rerunning Syft 1.33.0 and Trivy 0.66.0 (the versions they name; Trivy built from the tagged source) gives the same npm results as our pinned versions and the same Rust lists in 7 of 8 projects, so tool version does not explain either difference; in both Trivy versions the presence of Cargo.toml next to Cargo.lock reduces the packages listed (bat 181 to 149, dust 123 to 107), which is consistent with their lock only input but was not verified against their setup [V for the reruns, P for the explanation, results in docs/pilot_findings.md].
VEX and reachability. Hidden Dependencies (arXiv 2604.21278) shows on a synthetic Java benchmark around one vulnerability that Grype and CVE-Bin-Tool applied a VEX statement to no case, including the unreachable one, while Trivy applied it to all four, including three reachable ones; it measures consistency, not accuracy [read in full]. Shi et al. (VEX-Bench, arXiv 2609.08040) build 75 expert labelled cases (Go 30, Java 25, Python 20) to evaluate LLM agents on assessing the exploitability of dependency vulnerabilities; 70.7 percent are not affected and half of all cases are not reachable. As baselines Trivy and OSV-Scanner reach F1 of 36.1 and 40.5 percent on the full set, and on the 30 Go cases govulncheck completes only 19 (it needs the project to build) with precision 22.2 and recall 28.6 percent, against 28.6 and 85.7 percent for OSV-Scanner's reachability mode; their affected label includes configuration and environment conditions, and they note that a reachability tool shows a function in the call graph, not that the path is feasible [read in full]. Churakova, Ekstedt and Schmid (arXiv 2503.14388) compare seven tools that produce VEX style reports (Trivy, Grype, DepScan, Docker Scout, Snyk, OSV-scanner, Vexy; 25 configurations) on 48 container images from Docker Hub and find low consistency: the highest pairwise Jaccard index is 0.694 (Trivy against Grype), the similarity of the databases the tools query correlates with report similarity (Pearson 0.88), and they rule out SBOM quality as the explanation in that setting. They study the generation of lists of affected vulnerabilities, not the consumption of VEX statements [read in full]. Rosso et al. (SVS-TEST, SAC 2026, arXiv 2512.17710) test seven scanning tools (Bomber, Dependency-Track 4.12.0, dep-scan 5.4.6, Grype 0.87.0, snyk-cli, FOSSA 4.27.43, Vulert) with 16 crafted single component SBOMs in 8 scenarios and report silent failures: Grype and dep-scan use the CycloneDX version field, ignore the version in the purl and fail silently when the field is missing, Dependency-Track and snyk-cli use the version in the purl, Grype 0.87.0 did not look up components without a version, and in one embedded CycloneDX VEX case only Dependency-Track suppressed the vulnerability; they report 22,320 purl entries without a version in the Wild SBOM dataset and list a Grype change that lets empty versions meet version constraints (status not confirmed there) [read in full]. Our version carrier controls (section 4.5) extend this to Trivy, OSV Scanner and Grype 0.114.0, which reads the version field first, falls back to the purl, and reports 44 advisories for a versionless nats-server product against 14 at its tag; the relation to the Grype change they list is untested [P].
Position. We add, on real projects and with frozen data: findings, not only components, with every miss attributed; scanner behaviour toward version fields (extending SVS-TEST to Trivy, OSV Scanner and a newer Grype, with conflicting carriers and finding counts), profiles and file layout; VEX on real findings with a Go oracle, product versus module scope, and a cross product measurement; and a mapping to the regulation and BSI text. ## 8 Threats to validity and limits [V as statements about our work]
1. One version per tool, one snapshot (frozen 2026-10-30), Grype as the scoring scanner; OSV Scanner is not scored.
2. Popular open source projects with shared dependencies; the Go set holds 608 of 1061 reference findings, PyPI findings come from two lockfile projects, the Rust reference (47 findings) is concentrated in starship, and the npm set has 3 projects. The BSI probe covers the 43 SBOMs per generator of the pilot, older pin, Go, Maven and PyPI sets, not the Rust and npm sets.
3. Reachability and VEX results are Go only; the oracle (govulncheck) is static and has no independent check yet: manual labels 0 of 40 [P].
4. Truth: Maven shipped truth has up to 3 of 237 components in doubt; PyPI install truth reflects resolution on 2026-11-07 and drifts from the pinned lock (28 of 37 poetry packages, 29 of 33 ansible-lint packages resolve to other versions).
5. Several controls use one or two projects and a minimal SBOM, so they show a mechanism, not a rate.
6. BSI text read from a mirror; the regulation read from the EUR-Lex consolidated text, which has no legal effect. No legal interpretation is offered.
7. Method errors found and corrected during the work are listed in docs/pilot_findings.md; every table is generated from files in the repository.
8. Reachability scope: the first reachability run covered whole modules and overstated what is called in the shipped binaries; the figures are now product level for nine of ten Go applications, and k9s stays at module level only because its analysis exceeded the memory available.
9. Container scope: operating system packages of eight official images only, one build of each; software outside the package database (Go modules, Python packages, binaries) is excluded, cdxgen was not run on images, both scanners are one version at one database snapshot, 8 nginx findings reported by Trivy from an SBOM and not from the image have an unclear status, and the reference is built by the authors from the package database.

## 9 Discussion and future work [P]
An accurate component list is necessary but not sufficient: the identifier that carries the product's version, the graph from the product to the vulnerable component, the scope, and the form of a VEX statement each change what a scanner reports, and the three scanners read them differently. The regulation names no method for judging exploitability (Article 3(41) defines the term) and BSI names VEX and CSAF as the vehicle for the product level analysis. Future work: software in container images outside the package database, cdxgen on images, other ecosystems at scale, other tool versions, manual validation of the reachability oracle, a controlled study of scanners that read the product's identity from a different field.
